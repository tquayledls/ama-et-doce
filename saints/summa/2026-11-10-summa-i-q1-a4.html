Whether sacred doctrine is a practical science?

First Part, Question 1: The nature and extent of sacred doctrine, Article 4[2]

The School of Athens, Raphael, 1509–11, Stanza della Segnatura, Vatican[1]

Objection 1. It seems that sacred doctrine is a [[practical science::For Aquinas a "science" is organized, demonstrated knowledge. A practical science aims at action or making something (ethics, architecture); a speculative one aims only at knowing the truth.]]; for a practical science is that which ends in action according to [[the Philosopher::Aristotle. Metaph. is his Metaphysics, where he distinguishes knowing for its own sake from knowing for the sake of action.]] (Metaph. ii). But sacred doctrine is ordained to action: "Be ye doers of the word, and not hearers only" (James 1:22). Therefore sacred doctrine is a practical science.

Objection 2. Further, sacred doctrine is divided into the Old and the New Law. But law implies a [[moral science::Ethics: the study of how human acts ought to be ordered. Aquinas counts it as a practical science because its aim is right action.]] which is a practical science. Therefore sacred doctrine is a practical science.

On the contrary, Every practical science is concerned with human operations; as moral science is concerned with human acts, and [[architecture::Used as the standard example of a practical science: its whole point is to produce something, namely a building.]] with buildings. But sacred doctrine is chiefly concerned with God, whose handiwork is especially man. Therefore it is not a practical but a speculative science.

I answer that, Sacred doctrine, being one, extends to things which belong to different philosophical sciences because it considers in each the same [[formal aspect::The particular angle from which a subject is studied. Aquinas says one science can cover many topics if it views them all under one angle; here, as known by divine revelation.]], namely, so far as they can be known through divine revelation. Hence, although among the philosophical sciences one is [[speculative::Theoretical: from the Latin speculari, "to look at." A speculative science seeks to understand, not to act.]] and another practical, nevertheless sacred doctrine includes both; as God, by one and the same science, knows both Himself and His works. Still, it is speculative rather than practical because it is more concerned with divine things than with human acts; though it does treat even of these latter, inasmuch as man is ordained by them to the perfect knowledge of God in which consists eternal bliss. This is a sufficient answer to the Objections.
