Whether in God there are any accidents?

First Part, Question 3: The simplicity of God, Article 6[2]

Philosophy (Causarum Cognitio), Raphael, 1508–11, Stanza della Segnatura, Vatican[1]

Objection 1. It seems that there are [[accidents in God::An “accident” in Aristotle’s sense is not a mishap. It is any quality or feature, such as color, heat or wisdom, that belongs to a thing without being the thing itself. A man is a substance; his learning is an accident.]]. For substance cannot be an accident, as [[Aristotle says (Phys. i)::Aristotle (384–322 BC) is “the Philosopher” of the scholastics. The Physics is his work on nature and change.]]. Therefore that which is an accident in one, cannot, in another, be a substance. Thus it is proved that heat cannot be the [[substantial form of fire::In Aristotle’s physics, the substantial form is what makes a thing the kind of thing it is. The objection argues that heat is not the form of fire, since heat is a mere accident in other bodies.]], because it is an accident in other things. But wisdom, virtue, and the like, which are accidents in us, are attributes of God. Therefore in God there are accidents.

Objection 2. Further, in [[every genus there is a first principle::A genus is a broad class, such as substance, quantity or quality. The objection assumes each class has a first, highest member from which the others take their share.]]. But there are many "genera" of accidents. If, therefore, the primal members of these genera are not in God, there will be many primal beings other than God—which is absurd.

On the contrary, Every accident is in a subject. But God cannot be a subject, for "no simple form can be a subject", as [[Boethius says (De Trin.)::Boethius (c. 480–524), a Roman statesman and Christian philosopher, wrote short theological treatises, including On the Trinity. Aquinas cites him often.]]. Therefore in God there cannot be any accident.

I answer that, From all we have said, it is clear there can be no accident in God.

First, because a subject is compared to its accidents as [[potentiality to actuality::Potentiality is what a thing could become; actuality is what it already is. A subject receives its accidents, as a man who can learn becomes learned. God has nothing still to become (see I, q.2, a.3).]]; for a subject is in some sense made actual by its accidents. But there can be no potentiality in God, as was shown (I:2:3).

Secondly, because God is His own existence; and as Boethius says (Hebdom.), although every essence may have something superadded to it, this cannot apply to absolute being: thus a heated substance can have something extraneous to heat added to it, as whiteness, nevertheless absolute heat can have nothing else than heat.

Thirdly, because what is essential is prior to what is accidental. Whence as God is absolute primal being, there can be in Him nothing accidental. Neither can He have any essential accidents (as the capability of laughing is an [[essential accident of man::Aristotle’s example is the capacity to laugh. It is not part of what a man is, yet it follows necessarily from his rational nature. Thomas says even such a feature is out of the question in God.]]), because such accidents are caused by the constituent principles of the subject. Now there can be nothing caused in God, since He is the first cause. Hence it follows that there is no accident in God.

Reply to Objection 1. Virtue and wisdom are not predicated of God and of us [[univocally::“Univocally” means in exactly the same sense. Thomas holds that “wise” said of God and of man is not the same in meaning; in us wisdom is a quality we have, in God it is what He is.]]. Hence it does not follow that there are accidents in God as there are in us.

Reply to Objection 2. Since substance is prior to its accidents, the principles of accidents are reducible to the principles of the substance as to that which is prior; although God is not first as if contained in the genus of substance; yet He is first in respect to all being, outside of every genus.
