Whether God is altogether simple?

First Part, Question 3: The simplicity of God, Article 7[1]

The Separation of Light from Darkness, Michelangelo, 1511–12, Sistine Chapel ceiling, Vatican[2]

Objection 1. It seems that God is not [[altogether simple::“Simple” here does not mean easy. It means having no parts of any kind: nothing in God is added to, joined to or distinct from what God is.]]. For whatever is from God must imitate Him. Thus from [[the first being::Earlier, in I, q.2, a.3 (the Five Ways), Aquinas argued that there must be a first, uncaused source of all that exists. This is what he means by calling God “the first being.”]] are all beings; and from the first good is all good. But in the things which God has made, nothing is altogether simple. Therefore neither is God altogether simple.

Objection 2. Further, whatever is best must be attributed to God. But with us that which is composite is better than that which is simple; thus, chemical compounds are better than simple elements, and animals than the parts that compose them. Therefore it cannot be said that God is altogether simple.

On the contrary, [[Augustine says::St. Augustine of Hippo (354–430), the greatest of the Latin Fathers. Aquinas cites his work on the Trinity, De Trinitate.]] (De Trin. iv, 6,7): "God is truly and absolutely simple."

I answer that, The absolute simplicity of God may be shown in many ways.

First, from the previous articles of this question. For there is neither composition of quantitative parts in God, since He is not a body; nor composition of matter and form; nor does His nature differ from His "[[suppositum::Latin for “what stands underneath”: the individual, concrete someone who has a nature. Aquinas says that in God the person and the nature are not two things.]]"; nor His essence from His existence; neither is there in Him composition of [[genus and difference::In scholastic logic, a thing is defined by its genus (the wider kind, as “animal”) and its difference (what sets it apart, as “rational”). Aquinas has shown that God has no such definition.]], nor of subject and accident. Therefore, it is clear that God is nowise composite, but is altogether simple.

Secondly, because every composite is posterior to its component parts, and is dependent on them; but God is the first being, as shown above (I:2:3).

Thirdly, because every composite has a cause, for things in themselves different cannot unite unless something causes them to unite. But God is uncaused, as shown above (I:2:3), since He is the first efficient cause.

Fourthly, because in every composite there must be [[potentiality and actuality::Aristotle’s pair: actuality is what a thing is now; potentiality is what it could become. A composite has parts that can be joined or separated, so something in it is always still able to change.]]; but this does not apply to God; for either one of the parts actuates another, or at least all the parts are potential to the whole.

Fifthly, because nothing composite can be [[predicated of::To “predicate” is to say something is true of a thing, as in “this man is white.” Aquinas’s point is that no part of a composite is the whole.]] any single one of its parts. And this is evident in a whole made up of dissimilar parts; for no part of a man is a man, nor any of the parts of the foot, a foot. But in wholes made up of similar parts, although something which is predicated of the whole may be predicated of a part (as a part of the air is air, and a part of water, water), nevertheless certain things are predicable of the whole which cannot be predicated of any of the parts; for instance, if the whole volume of water is two cubits, no part of it can be two cubits. Thus in every composite there is something which is not it itself. But, even if this could be said of whatever has a form, viz. that it has something which is not it itself, as in a white object there is something which does not belong to the essence of white; nevertheless in the form itself, there is nothing besides itself. And so, since God is [[absolute form::Form, for Aquinas, is what makes a thing what it is. God is pure form, with no matter to receive it, and so is identical with His own act of existing.]], or rather absolute being, He can be in no way composite. [[Hilary implies::St. Hilary of Poitiers (c. 310–367), bishop and defender of the faith of Nicaea against the Arians, wrote On the Trinity.]] this argument, when he says (De Trin. vii): "God, Who is strength, is not made up of things that are weak; nor is He Who is light, composed of things that are dim."

Reply to Objection 1. Whatever is from God imitates Him, as caused things imitate the first cause. But it is of the essence of a thing to be in some sort composite; because at least its existence differs from its essence, as will be shown hereafter, (I:4:3).

Reply to Objection 2. With us composite things are better than simple things, because the perfections of created goodness cannot be found in one simple thing, but in many things. But the perfection of divine goodness is found in one simple thing (I:4:1 and I:6:2).
